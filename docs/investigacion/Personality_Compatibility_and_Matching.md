# Personality Compatibility and Matching

**Evidence synthesis:** 11 completed literature searches, reviewed November 1, 2026.

## Executive summary

The strongest overall conclusion is that personality matters in close relationships, but **matching people because their traits are similar has limited and inconsistent evidence as a way to improve relationship outcomes**. A person’s own traits and their partner’s traits often relate to satisfaction, support, conflict, and stability. Similarity usually adds little once those individual actor and partner effects are modeled.

Large dyadic studies illustrate the distinction. In 4,464 older couples followed across multiple waves, actor and partner Big Five traits explained more variance in well-being than the additional nonlinear terms used to test similarity; the one similarity result that remained relatively robust concerned women reporting more support when partners were similar in Agreeableness \[Sch18\]. In a separate sample of 1,294 couples, no Big Five trait- or facet-level similarity effect met the study’s full response-surface criteria, although actor and partner traits were associated with satisfaction \[Wei23\]. Nationally representative samples likewise found little incremental contribution from personality similarity to relationship satisfaction \[Dyr10\].

This does not mean all matching effects are zero. Some studies find small, trait-specific, or outcome-specific effects. But evidence that similarity predicts **who will be attracted to whom**, **who will form a relationship**, and **which established couples will be satisfied or remain together** comes from different designs and should not be treated as interchangeable. Assortative mating is clearly present in many domains; that alone does not show that similarity causes better relationships.

For a personality-based matching system, the evidence supports cautious, probabilistic claims rather than compatibility guarantees. Relationship processes such as attachment insecurity, trust, conflict, and communication appear more consistently connected to relationship quality than simple trait matching. Any system would also need independent validation, clear limits on sensitive-data use, and fairness measures that consider both sides of a match.

## Romantic similarity, attraction, and prediction

**Relationship quality.** The large-sample evidence favors individual trait levels over a general similarity benefit. In the Health and Retirement Study, actor and partner traits explained meaningful variance in well-being and relational outcomes, while the polynomial terms added relatively little; most apparent similarity patterns did not hold consistently across trait levels or gender \[Sch18\]. The 1,294-couple response-surface study likewise found no robust Big Five similarity effect for relationship or life satisfaction \[Wei23\]. These results support modeling both partners’ traits, but they do not support reducing a couple to a single similarity score.

**Attraction and preference matching.** Results from initial encounters are mixed: some speed-dating work found perceived similarity more informative than actual similarity, while a later dyadic response-surface study found little support for either \[Tid13, Hum23\]. A large registered study across 43 countries found a small distinctive preference-matching association with romantic evaluations. The corrected profile-matching estimate was $`\beta=.19`$, dropping to $`\beta=.11`$ when normative matching was included; the normative component was larger. The design relied on participants’ own descriptions of their targets and cannot show that matching caused attraction \[Eas24\].

**Prediction.** In a four-year personality-to-outcome study, elastic-net models explained 37% of later overall relationship-quality variance under repeated nested cross-validation. However, the sample was 192 people already in relationships, actor traits drove most prediction, and partner and similarity variables did not add significant predictive value \[Gro19\]. In a broader synthesis of 43 longitudinal dyadic datasets, individual-difference measures explained up to 21% of baseline satisfaction and 12% at follow-up; relationship-specific measures reported by the person whose satisfaction was predicted explained about 45% at baseline. Change over time was poorly predicted, with no analysis explaining more than 5% of satisfaction change \[Joe20\]. These are not direct tests of a dating algorithm: predicting a current partner’s satisfaction differs from predicting whether two strangers will form a good relationship.

## Partner resemblance and relationship formation

A meta-analysis of 22 traits found positive partner correlations across all included domains, but the magnitude depended strongly on the domain. Pooled correlations were about .08–.21 for Big Five personality, .53 for education, .57 for religiosity, and .58 for political values. Heterogeneity was high—the median $`I^2`$ was 87.5%—so pooled estimates should not be treated as universal constants \[Hor23b\].

Partner resemblance does not reveal its cause. Similarity may reflect initial selection, shared environments, convergence, or selective breakup. Longitudinal results are mixed: some studies point mainly to selection, while others find alignment over time for particular attitudes or behaviors \[Sch24, Hud24\]. Evidence involving same-sex couples is growing but remains smaller and more selective than the evidence for different-sex couples; studies using inferred or “putative” same-sex pairings require particular care in interpretation \[Hor25, Ste17\].

Friendship studies show the same need to separate processes. Network studies report selection on some traits, including Agreeableness, Extraversion, and Openness, while later socialization effects vary by trait and sample \[Sel10, Zal20\]. In a six-wave study of 903 Polish adolescents, value similarity predicted formation of some advice and trust ties, while socialization appeared in friendship and trust networks; neither process was uniform across values and tie types \[Cie24\]. Perceived similarity can also diverge from measured similarity, especially as new acquaintances form \[Sel09\].

## What personality dimensions contribute

The HEXACO findings give the clearest specific signal for Honesty–Humility. Across 11 preregistered studies with 5,677 participants, higher Honesty–Humility was generally associated with less dishonesty toward a romantic partner, using self-reports, a short diary, and behavioral deception tasks. The effects varied in size and some were attenuated when related Honesty–Propriety measures were included; the studies do not establish causality \[Rei23\]. In a dyadic sample of 200 couples, Honesty–Humility and other HEXACO traits were associated with different forms of jealousy, but the study was cross-sectional and nonrepresentative \[Top25\]. Evidence that HEXACO similarity itself improves couple outcomes remains sparse.

Attachment and interaction processes offer a stronger evidence base for relationship functioning than simple personality matching. Attachment anxiety and avoidance tend to relate to lower satisfaction, with actor effects often stronger than partner effects \[Can19, Li12\]. In 1,014 Dutch couples, attachment insecurity—especially avoidance—was more consistently related to satisfaction than attachment similarity. Similarity added 1.2 percentage points of explained variance for instability in the full sample, but did not improve the best-fitting full-sample satisfaction model \[Con21b\]. Communication and conflict patterns are likely mechanisms and targets for further study, though the available searches did not establish broad incremental validity beyond the full Big Five or HEXACO.

Friendship and romantic relationships should not be collapsed into one outcome. Values, interests, and humor may help select or maintain friendships, with the specific pattern changing by age and network tie \[Cie24, Kin19, Hun16\].

## Measures, types, and analysis choices

**Spanish-language measures.** Spanish HEXACO-PI-R/HEXACO-100 studies support the six-factor structure in Spain, and a Brazilian-Portuguese adaptation adds related evidence. The searches did not identify Spanish or Latin American validations of HEXACO-60 or the Brief HEXACO Inventory. Spanish Big Five IPIP validations are more available than Spanish HEXACO item pools \[Ron13, Rom15, Cos19, Cup19, Cup24\]. The 16-language HEXACO-100 study included Spanish and found broad configural and qualified metric invariance, but not scalar invariance; cross-language observed mean comparisons therefore need caution \[Thi19\].

A Spanish ECR-12 validation in six Chilean samples (N = 3,703) supported two attachment factors, with alpha estimates of .72–.83 for anxiety and .78–.89 for avoidance. Metric gender invariance was generally supported, with partial invariance in the gay/lesbian sample \[Guz20\]. This supports use in the studied Chilean contexts, not automatic equivalence across all Spanish-speaking populations.

**Enneagram and MBTI.** A systematic review of the Enneagram found mixed evidence for reliability and validity; factor studies generally recovered fewer than nine factors, and the review found no empirical derivation of all nine types through clustering \[Hoo20b\]. A 25-year synthesis of MBTI Form M studies found acceptable internal consistency but major gaps in recent test–retest and structural-validity research \[Erf25\]. Continuous MBTI scores retain more information than dichotomous categories; one comparison estimated that dichotomizing scores loses 26–32% of information \[Har94\]. Evidence for type-based relationship compatibility is limited for MBTI and was not identified for the Enneagram \[Hoo20b, Kon10, Yi11\].

**Similarity and dyadic methods.** Difference scores can hide whether an outcome reflects one partner’s level, the other’s level, their combination, or a nonlinear pattern. Polynomial regression and response-surface analysis make those components explicit and should be tied to a clear, testable definition of “similarity” \[Sch18b, Hum19\]. Profile measures also differ in what they encode: distance, rank or shape resemblance, and elevation can yield different answers. A comparison of 43 profile-similarity measures found that some, including Pearson correlation and the double-entry ICC, did not behave consistently across datasets \[Car24, Fur10b\]. APIM accounts for actor and partner effects and dyadic dependence; power should be planned for the intended model and effect rather than by a universal dyad-count rule. Simulation tools such as PowerLAPIM can support that planning, subject to their assumptions \[Ken06, Gis18, Laf22\].

## Inclusion, data quality, and matching-system ethics

Measurement invariance is not a formality: broad factor structure may hold while item thresholds or intercepts differ, making group mean comparisons less secure. Cross-cultural reviews and non-WEIRD survey studies document challenges from translation, response styles, interviewer effects, and context \[Don20, Laa19\]. Evidence specific to sexual- and gender-minority groups remains instrument-specific. Same-sex couple personality-similarity findings are also mixed and should not be generalized from small or indirect samples \[Asa23, Gyu05, Bar17c\].

Careless-response checks should use multiple indicators. In experimental comparisons, within-person consistency indices and some model-based indices performed better overall than longstring or intra-individual variability measures; performance depended on the task and screening benchmark \[Gol24\]. Acquiescence and social-desirability controls likewise need to fit the scale and population rather than being treated as universal corrections \[Ram10, Kre19\].

Dating recommendation is a two-sided allocation problem: a useful recommendation must have a chance of being accepted by both people, and exposure is limited. Simulated reciprocal-recommendation research shows a trade-off between maximizing total matches and spreading opportunity more evenly; a Nash-social-welfare approach reduced envy in simulations and offline evaluation, but was not tested as a live deployment \[Tom24\]. Fairness audits should therefore report mutual-match outcomes and exposure distribution, not only one-sided ranking accuracy. A dating-dataset study used opposite-gender interaction ratio as a proxy for user groups, but that proxy is not a direct measure of sexual orientation and the dataset used binary gender categories \[Zha24c\].

Privacy and safety are central, not secondary. In a U.S. exploratory study of closeted LGBTQ+ dating-service users (114 survey respondents and nine interviewees), 35.1% reported some privacy violation; participants described unwanted disclosure, image sharing, threats, and risks of being recognized offline \[Bou24b\]. Personality-based matching should avoid presenting trait scores as guarantees, minimize sensitive data collection, and make visibility and consent controls central to the design.

## Implications and evidence gaps

1.  **Do not equate resemblance with compatibility.** Partner similarity is common, especially for values and social attitudes, but the evidence that it independently improves satisfaction or stability is much weaker.
2.  **Separate the prediction target.** Attraction at first meeting, relationship formation, satisfaction in existing couples, and breakup are distinct outcomes. A model validated for one cannot be assumed to predict the others.
3.  **Compare matching against strong baselines.** Test whether partner traits or similarity improve prediction beyond each person’s own traits, current relationship processes, and normative desirability. Use held-out or external validation and report calibration and incremental variance explained.
4.  **Use dyadic designs and transparent similarity definitions.** Measure both partners, distinguish actual from perceived similarity, model actor and partner effects, and avoid interpreting a single profile score as a causal compatibility effect.
5.  **Treat subgroup validity as an empirical question.** Validate measures and recommendation outcomes across languages, cultures, gender identities, sexual orientations, and socioeconomic groups; do not infer identity from sparse behavior logs without explicit justification.
6.  **Build privacy and fairness into evaluation.** Report who receives exposure, who reciprocates, how outcomes differ across groups, and what sensitive data the system retains. Offline metrics and simulated fairness gains should be followed by carefully governed real-world tests.

The literature supports personality as one source of information about relationship behavior and satisfaction. It does not yet support deterministic personality typing or trait similarity as a reliable stand-alone method for assigning compatible partners. The most defensible approach is to treat compatibility predictions as uncertain, test their added value against simpler baselines, and evaluate them with dyadic, longitudinal, inclusive, and privacy-preserving designs.

## Evidence note

This synthesis combines the 11 completed deep-search summaries, which draw on scholarly metadata and abstracts, with full-text readings for selected central studies. The search results are broad and useful for mapping the field, but they are not a formal systematic review with independent screening and risk-of-bias assessment. Several findings—especially Enneagram psychometrics, actual versus perceived similarity in attraction, and Spanish short-form HEXACO validation—would benefit from further full-text review because key papers were not available as PDFs in the workspace.
