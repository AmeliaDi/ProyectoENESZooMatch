# In romantic couples, does partner similarity versus complementarity in personality traits (Big Five or HEXACO) predict relationship satisfaction, stability, or attraction? Prioritize meta-analyses and dyadic studies with large samples (N > 300 couples) that distinguish actual similarity from perceived similarity, and that use profile-similarity indices (elevation, scatter, shape) versus trait-by-trait analyses.

Across the largest and most rigorous dyadic studies, Big Five personality similarity between romantic partners does not meaningfully predict relationship satisfaction, stability, or initial attraction, explaining less than 0.5% of satisfaction variance in nationally representative samples once individual partner personality levels are controlled, and no evidence supports complementarity as a beneficial alternative; what predicts relationship quality is each partner's own and their partner's personality level—particularly for Neuroticism, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness—not how closely the two personalities match.

## Abstract

Across ten dyadic studies spanning cross-sectional, longitudinal, and speed-dating designs, the consistent finding from the largest and most methodologically rigorous studies is that Big Five personality similarity between romantic partners accounts for negligible variance in relationship satisfaction, stability, or initial attraction. In three nationally representative samples totaling over 23,000 married couples, couple similarity explained less than 0.5% of relationship satisfaction variance after controlling for actor and partner personality levels[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010]. A longitudinal RSA study of 4,464 couples corroborates this, showing that similarity contributes little beyond actor and partner effects and that, where second-order patterns emerge, they depend on the joint combination of both partners' trait levels rather than their mere resemblance[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018]. A large German panel study (n = 3,674 couples) does identify statistically significant profile-similarity effects on satisfaction (β ≈ 0.04), but these are smaller than individual-level predictors, and personality profile similarity did not significantly predict union stability[O. Becker, 2013]. For initial attraction, neither actual nor perceptual Big Five similarity predicted romantic interest in naturalistic speed-dating, while perceived partner trait levels did[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023]. No included study supports personality complementarity as a predictor of better outcomes.

Several important nuances qualify this overall null. Profile shape similarity (profile correlation) shows slightly more promise than trait-by-trait absolute difference scores[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008], and domain matters considerably: value and political-attitude similarity show more reliable associations with satisfaction than personality-trait similarity[S. Leikas et al., 2018], and life-goal and value profile similarity carry small indirect benefits for union stability that personality similarity does not[O. Becker, 2013]. The one robust longitudinal similarity effect is trait-specific—Agreeableness similarity predicting women's relational support over eight years[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018]—while a counterintuitive 12-year longitudinal study found that greater overall Big Five similarity predicted steeper negative satisfaction trajectories, particularly for Conscientiousness and Extraversion similarity among midlife couples[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]. Actor and partner personality levels, especially for Neuroticism, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness, consistently outperform similarity as predictors across studies[R. Weidmann et al., 2016]. Congruence between a person's ideal mate personality concept and their partner's actual personality predicted relationship outcomes better than either personality similarity or individual partner traits[M. Zentner, 2005], suggesting that idiosyncratic compatibility—rather than inter-partner resemblance—may be the more functionally relevant construct. No studies in this body of evidence address HEXACO personality.

The included studies span a wide range of sample sizes and methodological sophistication. The three largest studies—Dyrenforth et al. (2010)[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010], van Scheppingen et al. (2018)[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018], and Becker (2013)[O. Becker, 2013]—each include thousands of couples and use nationally representative or large panel datasets, providing the greatest statistical power to detect small effects. Humberg et al. (2023)[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023] contributes the only study directly targeting initial attraction in a naturalistic context, while Shiota and Levenson (2007)[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007] and Brandstätter et al. (2018)[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018] are substantially smaller, with 67 and 34–66 couples respectively; findings from these smaller samples warrant proportionally greater caution. Weidmann et al. (2016)[R. Weidmann et al., 2016], though framed as a meta-analysis, ultimately synthesizes evidence on rank-order stability of relationship satisfaction rather than directly estimating the effect of personality similarity on satisfaction levels, and so its contribution to the central research question is primarily contextual. Only three studies—van Scheppingen et al. (2018)[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018], Humberg et al. (2023)[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023], and Leikas et al. (2018)[S. Leikas et al., 2018]—employ Response Surface Analysis (RSA) in ways that allow genuine separation of similarity, elevation, and other second-order surface features. Zentner (2005)[M. Zentner, 2005] and Luo et al. (2008)[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008] are available only as abstracts, limiting assessment of their analytic details. None of the included studies addresses HEXACO personality, so all evidence concerns the Big Five framework or closely related constructs.

Across the largest and most methodologically robust studies, the pattern is consistent: personality similarity accounts for very little variance in relationship satisfaction once actor (own) and partner (spouse's) personality levels are controlled. Dyrenforth et al. (2010) show that similarity explains less than 0.5% of relationship satisfaction variance in three nationally representative samples[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010], and van Scheppingen et al. (2018) replicate this conclusion longitudinally in 4,464 couples using RSA, noting that most patterns depend on the particular combination of both partners' trait levels rather than on their similarity per se[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018]. Becker (2013) finds statistically significant but very small profile-similarity effects (β ≈ 0.04) across life goals, values, and personality[O. Becker, 2013], and explicitly concludes that individual characteristics predicted relationship outcomes better than similarity measures[O. Becker, 2013]. Leikas et al. (2018) add that personality-trait similarity in particular is unrelated to satisfaction, while value similarity (Self-Direction) and political-attitude similarity show domain-specific associations[S. Leikas et al., 2018]. The Weidmann et al. (2016) meta-analysis corroborates the primacy of actor and partner effects—especially for Neuroticism, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness—over similarity effects[R. Weidmann et al., 2016].

The dissenting signal in this literature comes from Brandstätter et al. (2018), whose five studies consistently find that greater Big Five profile distance predicts lower relationship satisfaction[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018]. However, these studies used samples of 34–66 couples[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018], making them the smallest in the review by a wide margin. The authors' key methodological contribution—combining profile distance with profile positivity as a suppressor—improves prediction, but the longitudinal associations with satisfaction change were inconsistent in direction and significance[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018]. The contrast between Brandstätter et al.'s positive similarity findings and the null or near-null findings in studies several orders of magnitude larger should be weighed accordingly.

A notable distinction in the extant literature is between trait-by-trait absolute difference scores and profile-level similarity measures. Luo et al. (2008) find that the profile correlation (shape similarity) made an independent contribution to marital satisfaction while the absolute difference score (level similarity) did not[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008]. Becker (2013) similarly finds that profile correlations rather than trait-specific discrepancies drove the significant associations[O. Becker, 2013]. RSA studies by van Scheppingen et al. (2018)[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018] and Leikas et al. (2018)[S. Leikas et al., 2018] push further by separating second-order surface parameters, revealing that the simplistic "more similar = more satisfied" conclusion misses complex joint-level dependencies. Van Scheppingen et al. in particular find that for most traits except neuroticism, the effect of similarity or dissimilarity on well-being depends on the level and combination of both partners' trait scores, not just their difference[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018].

Only Becker (2013) directly addresses union stability as an outcome. Profile similarity in life goals and values—but not in personality—showed small indirect beneficial effects on stability (i.e., reduced dissolution) mediated through relationship satisfaction[O. Becker, 2013]. Personality profile similarity did not significantly reach stability through this pathway[O. Becker, 2013]. This domain-specificity is important: the popular assumption that personality similarity stabilizes marriages finds only weak and indirect support even in one of the largest dyadic panel studies available.

The longitudinal evidence on satisfaction trajectories is particularly instructive because it challenges even the modest cross-sectional similarity advantages suggested by some studies. Shiota and Levenson (2007) find, in a 12-year longitudinal study, that greater Big Five similarity predicted steeper negative declines in marital satisfaction[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]. While this counterintuitive finding must be interpreted cautiously given the very small sample (N = 67 couples)[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007], it is consistent with a theoretical account emphasizing that similar partners amplify each other's dispositional tendencies over time rather than compensating for them. The age-group interactions are also worth noting: Conscientiousness and Extraversion similarity produced adverse effects specifically among midlife couples but not among older couples[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007], suggesting that the relevance of personality matching may depend on the life tasks confronting the couple at a given developmental stage.

Van Scheppingen et al. (2018)'s sole robust longitudinal similarity finding—that Agreeableness similarity predicted women's relational support across eight years[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018]—aligns with earlier meta-analytic evidence reviewed by Weidmann et al. (2016) highlighting Agreeableness as the trait most consistently linked (at the actor and partner level) to relationship quality[R. Weidmann et al., 2016].

Humberg et al. (2023) provide the only direct test of personality similarity and initial attraction, using dyadic RSA in a naturalistic speed-dating paradigm. The null result for both actual and perceptual similarity is notable precisely because the perceptual-similarity test is demanding: people hold similarity as an explicit mating preference, yet even perceiving one's partner as similar to oneself did not predict attraction[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023]. Instead, partner effects—perceiving the date as less neurotic and more extraverted, open, agreeable, and conscientious—drove attraction[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023]. This finding is consistent with the broader literature showing that partner personality levels matter more than similarity.

## The Role of Ideal Mate Personality Congruence

Zentner (2005) introduces a conceptually distinct predictor: congruence between an individual's ideal mate personality concept (IMPC) and the partner's actual or perceived personality. Over a 9-month prospective study, IMPC congruence predicted relationship outcomes better than either personality similarity between partners or the partner's individual personality traits[M. Zentner, 2005]. The Q-sort instrument showed individual variability in ideals alongside short-term consistency[M. Zentner, 2005], suggesting that IMPCs are reliable but idiosyncratic. Although the abstract does not specify sample size, effect sizes, or the exact relationship outcomes measured[M. Zentner, 2005], the findings raise the possibility that the weak similarity-satisfaction link in the broader literature partly reflects inadequate operationalization: what matters may not be whether partners resemble each other in absolute terms, but whether the partner's personality matches what that individual specifically seeks.

# Synthesis

## The Apparent Contradiction

The literature presents a superficially puzzling pattern. The very largest studies—Dyrenforth et al. (2010)[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010], van Scheppingen et al. (2018)[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018], and Becker (2013)[O. Becker, 2013]—converge on the conclusion that personality similarity accounts for little or no variance in relationship satisfaction and stability beyond actor and partner effects. Leikas et al. (2018) and Humberg et al. (2023) replicate this null for personality similarity specifically, whether in ongoing relationships or initial attraction[S. Leikas et al., 2018; Sarah Humberg et al., 2023]. Yet Brandstätter et al. (2018) consistently find, across five studies, that greater profile distance predicts lower satisfaction[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018], and Shiota and Levenson (2007) find that similarity is associated with more negative satisfaction trajectories[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]. Zentner (2005) finds that a particular form of congruence outperforms similarity[M. Zentner, 2005]. These tensions can be substantially resolved by examining methodological design, effect magnitude, and the nature of the similarity construct being tested.

## Study Quality and Sample Size

The most decisive consideration is sample size. Brandstätter et al.'s five studies[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018] collectively involved fewer couples than even the smallest of the three national samples in Dyrenforth et al.[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010], and Shiota and Levenson's 12-year study included only 67 couples[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]. Small samples generate imprecise and potentially spurious estimates. Both Dyrenforth et al.[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010] and van Scheppingen et al.[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018], with combined samples of tens of thousands of couples and longitudinal designs, should be weighted substantially more heavily in any evidence synthesis. The meta-analytic synthesis by Weidmann et al. (2016), though primarily concerned with stability of satisfaction rather than its determinants, corroborates the pattern that actor and partner effects dominate over similarity[R. Weidmann et al., 2016]. Becker's German Family Panel study[O. Becker, 2013] is the only large-N study to find statistically significant similarity effects, and even there the effects (β ≈ 0.04) are small enough to be of minimal practical importance and smaller than the individual-level predictors.

## Metric Distinctions: Profile Distance, Profile Correlation, and RSA

The conflict between positive findings in Brandstätter et al. (2018)[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018] and Luo et al. (2008)[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008] on one side, and null findings in most large studies on the other, partially reflects different operationalizations of similarity. Luo et al. find that the profile correlation (shape similarity) contributes independently to marital satisfaction, but the absolute difference score (level similarity) does not[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008]. Becker (2013) similarly finds that stereotype-adjusted profile correlations—which partial out mean-level elevation—drive whatever similarity effects emerge[O. Becker, 2013]. Brandstätter et al.'s profile distance measure conflates elevation, scatter, and shape[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018], potentially detecting an elevation (positivity) effect that the authors themselves operationalize separately as "positivity." Their finding that positivity and distance act as reciprocal suppressors[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018] suggests that what drives relationship satisfaction in their data may be the absolute level of positive personality traits (i.e., actor and partner effects for favorable traits) rather than inter-partner similarity per se—a result consistent with the broad consensus from larger studies.

RSA studies add further nuance. Van Scheppingen et al. (2018) find that for most Big Five traits except neuroticism, the surface relating both partners' scores to well-being is not a simple similarity ridge but a complex pattern in which the effect of similarity or dissimilarity depends on the joint level of both partners' traits[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018]. This means that asking "does similarity help?" is often the wrong question: whether similarity is beneficial, neutral, or harmful may depend on whether both partners are high or low on a given trait. Leikas et al. (2018) show further that even where similarity exists—as in conservative political attitudes, where conservative-conservative unions were most common[S. Leikas et al., 2018]—the satisfaction associations are non-linear and domain-specific rather than uniform across personality traits[S. Leikas et al., 2018].

## Domain Specificity: Personality vs. Values, Attitudes, and Ideals

A recurring moderator across studies is the domain of the similarity measure. Personality-trait similarity (Big Five) consistently shows weaker and less reliable associations with satisfaction than similarity in values, attitudes, or ideals. Leikas et al. (2018) find that political-attitude similarity predicted women's satisfaction and Self-Direction value similarity predicted both partners' satisfaction, while personality-trait similarity was entirely unrelated[S. Leikas et al., 2018]. Becker (2013) finds that profile similarity in life goals and values had small positive associations with satisfaction, whereas personality profile similarity—although statistically significant—was numerically identical and the corresponding indirect stability effect was nonsignificant[O. Becker, 2013]. Zentner (2005) goes further, showing that congruence between ideal mate personality concepts and the actual partner outperforms personality similarity as a predictor[M. Zentner, 2005]. Taken together, these findings suggest that homogamy in values and compatibility with individually held ideals may be the more functionally meaningful constructs, and that the search for personality-similarity effects specifically has been directed at a relatively weak signal.

## Temporal Dynamics and Life Stage

Shiota and Levenson's counterintuitive finding—that greater Big Five similarity predicted steeper negative satisfaction trajectories over 12 years[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]—may reflect a mechanistic reality rather than a statistical artefact, even given the small sample. One plausible mechanism is that personality-similar couples lack complementary resources for coping with the challenges of midlife (e.g., both partners being similarly low in flexibility may amplify rather than buffer stressors), consistent with the finding that the adverse effects of Conscientiousness and Extraversion similarity were specific to midlife rather than older couples[M. Shiota & R. Levenson, 2007]. Van Scheppingen et al.'s (2018) longitudinal finding that Agreeableness similarity robustly predicted women's relational support across eight years[M. V. van Scheppingen et al., 2018] is the strongest positive evidence for a lasting personality similarity effect, and it is notably trait-specific rather than reflecting global similarity. The Brandstätter et al. (2018) longitudinal component is weaker: satisfaction level and trajectory predictions from distance and positivity differed in direction and magnitude across studies[H. Brandstätter et al., 2018], indicating that the cross-sectional pattern does not straightforwardly extend to change over time.

## What Can Be Concluded

For relationship satisfaction in established couples, the weight of evidence from large, methodologically rigorous studies indicates that personality similarity (Big Five) accounts for a negligible share of variance—less than 0.5% in the most stringently controlled analyses[Portia S. Dyrenforth et al., 2010]—while actor and partner personality levels, particularly for Neuroticism, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness, are the dominant personality-based predictors[R. Weidmann et al., 2016]. Profile-level shape similarity may carry a small incremental contribution beyond trait-by-trait absolute differences[Shan-Hong Luo et al., 2008; O. Becker, 2013], but these effects remain substantively modest. For relationship stability, the personality-similarity literature provides no reliable direct evidence; what indirect effects exist are mediated through satisfaction and appear specific to life-goal and value similarity rather than personality per se[O. Becker, 2013]. For initial attraction, personality similarity—actual or perceptual—appears irrelevant[Sarah Humberg et al., 2023], with perceived partner trait levels being the operative predictor. The claim that personality complementarity promotes relationship quality finds no support in any included study; no study reports that trait dissimilarity predicts better outcomes. The most productive reframing of the research question may be Zentner's (2005): rather than asking whether partners resemble each other, asking whether each partner's personality matches what their specific partner ideally seeks[M. Zentner, 2005] may capture the functional compatibility construct that global similarity indices fail to operationalize.
